Abstract

Search performance deterioration can motivate a content review, but refresh queues must be evaluated without leaking future outcomes. We studied 158,549 anonymized content–client observations across 46 clients using March 2026 Google Search Console signals to predict whether April impressions fell more than 20%. A random forest and a visibility-only heuristic were compared on the same client-disjoint holdout containing 23,235 pages from 12 clients. The forest reached Precision@50 of 80.0% versus 36.0% for the heuristic, with ROC AUC 0.700 versus 0.570. These findings support further testing of prioritization scores, not claims of causal improvement from refreshing content.

1. Introduction

Content operations involve choosing which pages deserve review. Search impressions are noisy and may decline for many reasons unrelated to content quality. This study asks whether information available on March 31 can prioritize pages likely to experience a substantial decline in April. The purpose is decision support for a limited review queue, not attribution of future losses or estimation of the effect of interventions.

2. Data and Research Question

The feature window was March 1–31, 2026, and the outcome window was April 1–30, 2026. One observation is one pseudonymized client–content pair with observed Google Search Console tracking in both windows. The export contained 158,549 observations from 46 clients. Observed overall proxy-label prevalence was 47.8%. The outcome was 1 precisely when April impressions were strictly below 80% of March impressions. An inner join between March and April aggregates excluded pages with no April observation; this limits the population to continuously observable pages and may understate failure or disappearance.

3. Features and Leakage Controls

The predictors are March impressions, March clicks, March CTR expressed in percent, mean reported search position, and count of days with positive impressions. April impressions are used only to construct the target after the fact. Client and content identifiers are retained for grouping and checks, never as model inputs. No future performance, downstream intervention decision, or label-derived variable enters the fitted model. The month boundary is a real decision-time boundary, though one month pair does not prove temporal robustness.

4. Baseline and Model

The transparent baseline ranks held-out pages by descending log(1 + March impressions): pages with greater current visibility are reviewed first. This is a simple visibility-only benchmark, not the separate Week 4 heuristic from the starter dataset. The model is a random forest of 150 trees with minimum leaf size 10, square-root feature sampling, and fixed seed 42; numeric median imputation is available in the pipeline. Scores are positive-class probabilities. Parameters were chosen as a fixed exploratory setup, not tuned on the test clients.

5. Evaluation Design

A single seeded GroupShuffleSplit holds out entire clients (34 train, 12 test), yielding 135,314 training pages and 23,235 evaluation pages. No client appears in both sets. Both methods rank the identical evaluation population and are assessed using global Precision@20, @50, @100, and @500, ROC AUC, and average precision. The observed held-out prevalence is 47.0%. A supplementary per-client Precision@50 is averaged over clients with at least 50 eligible pages (7 clients).

6. Results

Global Precision@20 was 80.0% for the forest versus 55.0% for the baseline; Precision@50 was 80.0% versus 36.0%; Precision@100 was 73.0% versus 35.0%; and Precision@500 was 70.2% versus 37.2%. ROC AUC was 0.700 versus 0.570, and average precision was 0.636 versus 0.492. Macro per-client Precision@50 among eligible clients was 49.1% for the forest and 23.1% for the benchmark. Tree impurity-based feature importance (not causal explanation) ranked mean position (0.323) and impressions (0.292) highest.

7. Interpretation and Limitations

The improvement in ranking quality warrants additional validation, but it must not be interpreted as a lift in business outcomes. High global top-k precision can disproportionately represent large clients. Only 12 clients were held out, with 7 having at least 50 pages. The inner join to April conditions results on continued observability; March pages absent from April are not classified as zeros. The label is a thresholded ratio and may behave erratically for low-impression pages. A single March–April period cannot show robustness to seasonality, search updates, or evolving client composition. Impurity feature importance can mislead with correlated or differently distributed variables. No statistical uncertainty intervals or randomized intervention study are reported.

8. Action Playbook

For a pilot, score March-observed pages after the month closes; sort by model probability, then route the highest-ranked pages for a human diagnostic review. Before intervening, verify tracking, traffic volume, site changes, query mix, and whether the drop is seasonal or technical. Maintain an audit of reviewed pages and reviewer recommendations. Evaluate prospective outcomes on newly held-out months and clients before deployment; to estimate whether refresh actions cause improvements, use a randomized or carefully controlled experiment. Do not imply that the model identifies the reason for a decline.

9. Reproducibility and Conclusion

The companion script run_capstone.py recreates this comparison from a user-held CSV using a documented fixed random seed, client-level partition and five pre-outcome features. The pseudonymized data file, identifiers, and row-level predictions must remain out of public GitHub Pages and the repository unless FlyRank explicitly permits redistribution. A public deployment should contain only the aggregate report and sanitized code. The demonstrated result is improved retrospective prioritization for an observable population, with prospective and causal evaluation still required.
